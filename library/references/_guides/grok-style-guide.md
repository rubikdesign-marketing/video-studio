# Grok-folder short-video style guide

## Scope and provenance

This guide synthesizes ten locally supplied vertical videos stored in a folder named “Grok.” That folder name is a sample label, not proof that Grok commissioned, published or approved any clip. Several speakers say “Grok Bot,” “Grockbot” or “Grockbox,” and some claim a SpaceX AI affiliation; those spellings and affiliations are not independently verified by the files. At least two videos visibly include sponsorship disclosures, and some may be creator demos, promotional interviews or sponsored posts. Read the patterns below as characteristics of this supplied sample, not as a definitive description of official company output.

The folder contains ten 360 × 640 vertical videos, ranging from about 35.5 to 129.9 seconds. Four run under one minute and six run longer than one minute; the median is approximately 81.7 seconds. All ten show people and some form of product or interface context. Captions or subtitles are visible in nine of the ten sampled videos. Seven appear to use a spoken comment-to-get-access CTA; three end without one clearly established in the transcript. These counts describe this collection only. Each clip has a separate transcript file generated locally with MLX Whisper small. Treat quotations as machine-recognized speech and verify proper nouns, numbers and unclear endings against the source before publication.

## Patterns that work across the sample

**Translate the AI concept into a job.** The clearest clips name an outcome that viewers already understand: find job listings (`105…`, 9.52s), convert syllabi into a calendar (`112958…`, 12.40s), compare menu sales (`967…`, 36.76s), or summarize ad activity (`286…`, 0–8.84s). “Bots” becomes easier to picture when each has a role, input and output. The workplace montage's framing as “four new teammates” (`217…`, opening frames) and the creator's “team of bots” explanation (`177…`, 32.52s) both use this teammate metaphor.

**Pair claims with a visible interface.** Every video includes interface or product context, but the strongest sequences connect a screen to the spoken task. In `112958…`, the agent directory gives way to chat, calendar blocks and a grade breakdown. In `286…`, the ad spend question appears as a phone conversation, followed by a readable summary card and a later role map. The student and ad examples let a viewer understand what changed. By contrast, the trip montage (`112809…`) has memorable scenery but small chat screens; the screen suggests a response without making reservations or outcomes easy to inspect. Use a screen crop large enough to read, and hold on the decisive output.

**Name the role and repeat it consistently.** Distinct bot names and icons help viewers remember responsibilities: Lucy/Riley/Jeffrey/Karen in `105…`; Scout/Form/Brew in `108…`; Manny/Stud/Keys in `177…`; Marge/Ditto/Velcro/Jenny in `286…`. The interface labels act as chapter headings. However, ASR variants and occasional mismatch matter: `105…` UI appears to show Hermela while transcript hears Carmela; `108…` UI shows Brew while ASR hears “Brub”; `286…` transcript hears “Delcro” where role graphics suggest Velcro. Confirm labels visually and in source audio before turning them into a guide or ad copy.

**Use a human setting to show relevance.** Creator-led clips keep a person in frame while UI overlays sit on or beside them. Campus, home desk, car, cafe and travel locations make the task feel situated. `967…` uses coffee-making b-roll as a tactile bridge to POS data; `112958…` uses campus exteriors to set up academic organization; `108…` uses a home desk, marketplace listings and a coffee order to convey day-to-day work. These scenes help with context, but they do not independently verify customer status, business results or affiliation.

**Make the hook a concrete tension or curiosity gap.** The sample's hooks include a numbered list with a secrecy claim (`105…`), money and burnout (`177…`), a specific road-trip problem (`112809…`), fear of a missed exam (`112958…`), and a small business operator's identity (`967…`). Strong hooks establish what is at stake before the product explanation. A hook can be spoken, displayed, or both; in `107…`, the visible “Grok bot is literally insane” differs from the spoken “If you're a young guy with motion…”. When transcribing style, keep those channels distinct.

**Choose a CTA that matches the format.** Seven samples appear to ask for a comment keyword to receive access or a link; examples include `105…` at 32.44s, `107…` transcript ending, `112809…` at 49.80s, `177…` visible “Comment ‘Grok’,” `180…` visible “Comment ‘Grok’,” and `286…` at 127.66s. The coffee interview and workplace montage close on branding or an interview exchange without a clear comment CTA. For creator posts, comment-to-receive can be a simple next step, but put the same keyword in the spoken close and final on-screen text. Do not infer an unseen CTA from a logo.

## Exceptions and risks to preserve

The collection is not uniformly “creator UGC.” The office montage `217…` uses several people, controlled lighting, shallow focus, scripted-looking interfaces and a clean logo end card. The cafe story `967…` resembles a mini-documentary and relies on a named operator. The health video `180…` is a dark, polished product tour with an animated body illustration rather than a casual screen demo. Conversely, the travel sample `112809…` favors location footage over readable product proof. These are useful variations, not evidence of a single approved brand standard.

Some clips make high-impact claims: `180…` says bots manage a family's health, monitor wearables, adjust training and schedule a doctor; `286…` describes ad-budget changes and public social posting; `105…` claims agents send job applications, negotiate full refunds and buy tickets; `108…` claims agents submit business inquiries and place an order. Treat these as attributed speaker statements. Interface screens do not establish clinical validation, successful transactions, reliability, consent, or business performance. For sensitive actions, show permissions and human review. `286…` explicitly says uncertain community replies are drafted for approval; that is a useful boundary to keep. Sponsorship disclosure is visible in `112958…` and `177…`; preserve disclosures and do not imply official company authorship from the folder name or a speaker's SpaceX AI statement.

## Practical production templates

### 15 seconds: one task, one output

- **0–2s:** State a recognizable problem or outcome: “I had three course deadlines hidden across PDFs.”
- **2–4s:** Show the one bot or setup used; say what input it receives.
- **4–10s:** Show the actual workflow in one readable capture. Keep the most important output on screen long enough to understand.
- **10–13s:** State the practical result with a precise, supportable claim; separate a demo from a verified result.
- **13–15s:** One CTA or next step, shown and spoken with the same wording.

### 30 seconds: hook, proof, takeaway

- **0–3s:** Open on a specific tension, surprising fact, or short curiosity hook.
- **3–7s:** Introduce the person and task in one sentence.
- **7–18s:** Show a clear input → bot action → visible output sequence; use one agent and one screen.
- **18–25s:** Explain what changed for the user and what still requires their judgment.
- **25–30s:** Summarize the benefit, preserve any sponsorship disclosure, then give one CTA.

### 60 seconds: mini case story

- **0–4s:** State the problem and stakes.
- **4–12s:** Establish the operator and context with a concise on-camera line or a short location montage.
- **12–25s:** Show the setup and source material; explain one bot role.
- **25–42s:** Show the action and a readable, verifiable result. If there are multiple roles, show a clear handoff rather than listing features.
- **42–52s:** Share a supportable result or lesson and distinguish speaker-reported outcomes from independently verified evidence.
- **52–60s:** Recap, include disclosure, and deliver a short CTA.

## Production checklist

1. Write the hook as a single sentence. Decide whether it is spoken, on-screen, or both; keep exact wording consistent if both.
2. Choose one viewer and one concrete task. Give each bot one plain-language role and show how it receives input.
3. Capture a real, permissioned input and output. Blur personal data, customer details and account identifiers. Avoid fabricated performance numbers or label illustrative demo data.
4. Frame the host and product together without covering faces, captions or controls. Keep UI panels large enough for a phone viewer.
5. Use captions for sound-off viewing. Keep them short, high contrast and out of the main interface area. Don't rely on tiny native UI text to carry the story.
6. Connect each claim to visible proof. Show the action, the result and relevant human review. For public posting, spending, housing, health or other high-impact tasks, be clear about permission, consent and approval boundaries.
7. Check agent names, interface labels, numbers, locations and comment keywords against the source clip. Local ASR can mistranscribe proper nouns; the transcript is not on-screen text or independent verification.
8. Make the CTA concrete and accurate. If the offer is a link, setup guide or early access, say which one. Keep the comment keyword visible, readable and identical to the spoken version.
9. Preserve required sponsor disclosures. Treat creator statements about company affiliation as claims unless independently established.
10. Review the finished clip at phone size and without sound. Confirm that the story still makes sense, the evidence can be read, and the closing action is clear.

This guide is an expert synthesis of the ten supplied samples. It describes reusable creative techniques and evidence limits; it does not assert the official Grok brand voice or validate the product claims in any clip.
