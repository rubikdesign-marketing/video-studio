# ElevenLabs video style guide from the supplied sample

## Scope and evidence

This guide synthesizes nine user-supplied MP4s in the folder labeled ElevenLabs. The folder name is a supplied label, not proof that ElevenLabs published, sponsored, approved, or paid for every clip. Some videos look like creator endorsements, tutorials, staged interviews, or product showcases. This guide describes recurring choices in this sample, not a definitive statement of the company’s full brand strategy.

The clips are 19.5–66.8 seconds long, with a median of about 30.0 seconds. Eight are 360 × 640 portrait files; the sci-fi showcase is a 360 × 450 crop. Three-second still sampling supports broad visual sequence analysis, while machine-generated transcripts support timecoded speech analysis. Exact pacing, cut count, transition behavior, sound design, vocal quality, and authenticity require full playback. Product, performance, language-count, quality, and ranking claims remain claims made in the videos unless independently verified.

## Recurring creative patterns

**1. Start with a use case or result.** The sample favors a concrete reason to keep watching over a brand preamble. The ad-variation video opens with a strategic statement—“The key to a successful ad campaign is variance” (1015…, 0.00–6.24s). The avatar video begins with “I now have five avatars creating endless content for my brand” (2180…, 0.00–3.34s). The audiobook conversation begins with a time-specific question about making an audiobook in “like three hours” (9553…, 0.00–3.84s). The street interview asks about side hustles (9236…, 0.00–3.28s). Across these examples, the opening either names a pain, promises an output, or creates a curiosity gap. The best replication is to choose one of those jobs and say it plainly in the first few seconds.

**2. Make the product a means to a specific outcome.** The strongest workflow stories translate features into a recognizable task: change a voiceover line without rerecording (4541…, 21.40–27.60s), turn a document into an audiobook (1380…, 10.64–15.20s), or connect a brief and avatar to batch content (2180…, 17.18–20.76s). Voice, avatar, and generative features become easier to grasp when shown as steps in a creator’s job. The short cinematic showcase (1683…) is the exception: it relies on aspirational results and calls itself “one prompt away” (16.50–23.00s), but does not show a prompt or interface in the sampled frames. That can sell a creative ceiling, while a single process proof would make product attribution clearer.

**3. Interleave people, interface, and outputs.** At least six clips put a presenter or conversational subject at the center, then overlay or cut to interface screens. The pattern appears in the Flows explainer (1015…), avatar tutorial (2180…), voice cloning tutorial (2512…), retake workflow (4541…), creator workflow (8940…), and audiobook conversation (9553…). UI inserts give operational credibility; a person gives the workflow a human setting. The more effective balance is to show the exact screen related to the spoken step and make it large enough to read on a phone. Small UI is a recurring weakness, especially where the viewer is asked to infer a feature from a quick insert.

**4. Captions are structural.** Most samples use open captions that track the spoken explanation, usually bold white words with a dark edge or shadow. The avatar and retake videos use short, low-positioned all-caps words at sampled moments. The audiobook conversation at 9553… places black all-caps words inside small white rounded cards. The voice cloning tutorial has a more crafted opening: a display title, yellow hand-drawn arrow, and “This is not me” note (2512…, 0–4s). Captions can carry the story when playback is muted, but avoid fragmenting every phrase into a word if the key proposition becomes hard to read. Keep text within safe margins and avoid placing it over the face or critical UI controls.

**5. Use concrete proof, with careful attribution.** The sample repeatedly uses visible evidence: an audiobook on a phone (1380…, around 12s), avatar selection and a create screen (2180…, 6–18s), voice creation / dubbing screens (2512…, 20–34s), a voice waveform and settings (4541…, 27–45s), and analytics inserts (8940… and 9236…). Several clips also mention high view counts or production savings. Those numbers are not independently verified here. When reusing a creator result, state who is speaking, what period the number covers, and whether the result is an example or a guaranteed outcome. Avoid turning a testimonial into an implied product promise.

**6. End with one clear action.** Every clip has some form of next step in its transcript, but the CTA style varies: visit a URL (1015…, 22.00–26.16s; 9236…, 20.68–24.32s), start today (1380…), try free (1683…, 23.00–27.50s; 8940…, 26.76–30.30s), sign up free (2180…, 25.06–25.90s), comment for a guide (2512…, 39.26–42.76s), use a creator link (4541…, 59.28–66.64s), or check it out (9553…). Choose one action that matches the clip’s goal. A guide comment is useful when the content is tutorial-led; a trial CTA suits a product-led demo. Keep any offer, price, or free claim current and accurate.

## Visual and production direction

The dominant visual mode is creator-led and comparatively accessible: a home office, studio, sidewalk, or real production room rather than a uniform brand set. Wardrobe and location help differentiate pieces. Bright neutral rooms support product UIs; dark interfaces provide contrast; a warm office grounds a creator workflow. The cinematic sample (1683…) breaks the pattern with dark metal, moon scenes, spacecraft, and blue light. Its high-contrast spectacle is useful when the output itself is the proof.

For a small team, an efficient production approach is an **INFERENCE** from the finished clips: record a clear presenter section, capture only the relevant product screens, gather one or two real-world b-roll examples, then intercut them with captions. This is a likely workflow, not a verified description of original crews or software. For each interface insert, plan a readable crop and a pause long enough for a phone viewer to understand the action. If the claim concerns audio realism, include a real audio sample and verify it by listening; still images and ASR cannot establish sound quality.

## Adaptable script templates

**15 seconds: one proof point.**

- 0–2s: State one specific problem or result: “Still rerecording every small script change?”
- 2–5s: Name the product action: “Clone your voice once, then type the revised line.”
- 5–11s: Show one legible interface step and one output example.
- 11–13s: State the qualified benefit: “That can save a full retake for small edits.”
- 13–15s: One CTA: “Try it free” or “Comment for the setup guide.”

This condenses the strongest problem → product step → output pattern from 4541… and 2512…. Avoid promising exact time saved unless measured.

**30 seconds: a compact workflow.**

- 0–3s: Start with a specific job, result, or question.
- 3–8s: Explain the pain in one sentence and show the current method.
- 8–18s: Demonstrate two or three steps in the product UI. Keep each screen connected to the narration.
- 18–24s: Show the output and explain what changed.
- 24–27s: Give one reason it matters to this audience.
- 27–30s: End on one CTA and any required qualification.

This fits the avatar workflow (2180…) or audiobook flow (9553…). If using a metric, show its source and period and identify it as a creator result.

**60 seconds: a credible creator story.**

- 0–4s: Hook with the repeat-work problem or a qualified outcome.
- 4–15s: Describe the old workflow and where it broke down.
- 15–30s: Show the product setup and the exact step that removes friction.
- 30–42s: Demonstrate the output, including a before / after when possible.
- 42–52s: Explain who benefits and where the workflow has limits.
- 52–60s: Restate the concrete value and give one CTA.

The longer retake story (4541…) suggests room to build context, but its transcript includes duplicated / uncertain ASR passages; a new version should keep its core pain point and remove repetition. For a case story, separate the creator’s personal metric from any general product claim.

## Production checklist

- Write one sentence for the audience, use case, and outcome before filming.
- Choose a spoken hook and a visually distinct opening; confirm both work without relying on a three-second sample.
- Capture the actual product screen at a size that is readable in a 360-pixel-wide preview.
- Show a source object, interface action, and finished output when the story is a transformation.
- Use captions for the important spoken point; check safe margins, contrast, and legibility on a phone.
- Verify proper nouns, numbers, language counts, superlatives, pricing, and “free” offers against current product facts.
- Attribute testimonials and performance metrics to the person and the relevant time period; do not imply that the product guarantees the result.
- For voice or music claims, listen to the real output and include a clear sample.
- End with a single CTA appropriate to the video’s job.
- Review the complete export with sound before publishing; frame sampling and speech recognition are research aids, not substitutes for playback.

## What this sample suggests

The shared pattern is not one fixed visual identity but a repeatable explanation shape: an accessible person or concrete result earns attention; the product appears as a step in a familiar job; an output makes the promise visible; and a CTA closes the story. The most useful creative test is to compare a feature-first version with a problem-first version, while keeping the same UI proof and CTA. Measure completion and qualified action separately, since spectacle, testimonial trust, and tutorial utility may attract different viewers. Treat those tests as recommendations, not findings about which original clip performed best; no post-level performance data was supplied.
